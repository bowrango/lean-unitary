\begin{abstract}
The block-ZXZ decomposition of Krol and Al-Ars synthesizes an arbitrary $n$-qubit unitary with
$\tfrac{22}{48}4^n-\tfrac32 2^n+\tfrac53$ CNOT gates, the best known count for an exact analytic
method. We show that every recursion node admits one further CNOT cancellation. A free single-qubit
gate $u$ on the top qubit at each node can always be chosen so that the input-side multiplexed
rotation folds two CNOTs into the central factor instead of one. A parity rule for
one angle and the intermediate value theorem for the other prove the choice exists. The result
is $\tfrac{21}{48}4^n-\tfrac32 2^n+2$ CNOTs, i.e.\ $18$ for a general three-qubit unitary
(previously $19$) and $(4^{n-2}-1)/3$ fewer than block-ZXZ for every $n\ge 3$.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{The synthesis problem}

A quantum algorithm is ultimately compiled into a circuit of hardware gates. The standard
target is the gate set $\{\text{CNOT}\}\cup U(2)$. Here single-qubit gates are comparatively
cheap and accurate, so the number of CNOTs is the usual measure of circuit cost. For a generic
$n$-qubit unitary $U\in U(2^n)$ the question is how few CNOTs suffice to realize $U$
\emph{exactly}, for every $U$. A parameter count gives a lower bound: $U(2^n)$ has dimension
$4^n$, and each CNOT can add at most four parameters to the circuit once the surrounding
single-qubit gates are accounted for. Hence~\cite{smb}
\[
c_{\min}(n)\ \ge\ \big\lceil \tfrac14(4^n-3n-1)\big\rceil \approx \tfrac{12}{48}\,4^n .
\]
Known constructions attain this only for $n\le2$ (three CNOTs suffice for any two-qubit
unitary). For $n\ge3$ the gap sits in the \emph{leading coefficient}: the best constructions
cost $a\cdot4^n+O(2^n)$ with $a>\tfrac{12}{48}$. Lowering $a$ is the only way to gain a constant
fraction of the circuit for large $n$.

\subsection{State of the art: QSD and block-ZXZ}

The best analytic methods are recursive. They split off the top qubit $t=q_{n-1}$, write $U$ in
terms of four $(n-1)$-qubit unitaries and three \emph{multiplexed rotations}, and recurse down to
two-qubit leaves. A multiplexed rotation $\Delta(\theta)=\sum_j|j\rangle\langle j|\otimes
R_z(\theta_j)$ applies a different $R_z$ rotation to $t$ for each basis state $j$ of the lower
register. With $n-1$ controls it costs $D=2^{n-1}$ CNOTs in Gray-code form~\cite{mottonen}.
Multiplexors arise from \emph{demultiplexing}: every block-diagonal
$U_1\oplus U_2$ (meaning $U_1$ when $t=0$ and $U_2$ when $t=1$) factors as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:demux}
U_1\oplus U_2=(I\otimes V)\,\Delta(\theta)\,(I\otimes W),\qquad
U_1U_2^\dagger=V\operatorname{diag}(e^{-i\theta_j})V^\dagger ,
\end{equation}
so the angles $\theta_j$ are the eigenphases of $U_1U_2^\dagger$ up to sign.

The quantum Shannon decomposition (QSD)~\cite{smb} obtains the four sub-unitaries from a
cosine--sine decomposition. With its optimizations it uses $\tfrac{23}{48}4^n-\tfrac32
2^n+\tfrac43$ CNOTs. The block-ZXZ decomposition~\cite{zxz} replaces the cosine--sine step by the
factorization
\begin{equation}\label{eq:zxz}
U=(A_1\oplus A_2)\,(H\otimes I)\,(I\oplus B)\,(H\otimes I)\,(I\oplus C),\qquad
C=-i\,U_X^\dagger U_Y ,
\end{equation}
where $X$ and $Y$ are the top-row $D\times D$ blocks of $U$ and $X=S_XU_X$, $Y=S_YU_Y$ are their
polar decompositions. Demultiplexing the three block-diagonal factors by~\eqref{eq:demux} gives
the node of Fig.~\ref{fig:node}.

Its CNOT savings come from \emph{folds}. The last CNOT $\CX(q,t)$ of an outer multiplexor
(control $q$, target $t$) meets the Hadamard of the central factor. There
$(H\otimes I)\,\CX(q,t)=\CZ(q,t)\,(H\otimes I)$, and $\CZ(q,t)=I\oplus Z_q$ is itself
block-diagonal, so it is absorbed into the central factor before that factor is demultiplexed.
This removes one CNOT from each outer multiplexor. Two-qubit leaves cost $3$ CNOTs, or $2$ up to
a diagonal that migrates into the next leaf. Together these give
$c_{\rm ZXZ}(n)=4c_{\rm ZXZ}(n-1)+3\cdot2^{n-1}-5$, i.e.\ $\tfrac{22}{48}4^n-\tfrac32 2^n+\tfrac53$,
the best known analytic count. It is $19$ CNOTs for three qubits (QSD: $20$).

Numerical optimization does better on small registers. Brick-wall templates reach the lower
bound for $n\le5$ in numerical experiments~\cite{brickwall}, and gradient methods find
near-optimal circuits~\cite{squander}. These methods give no formula for the parameters and no
guarantee that every $U$ is reached, and the universality of the lower-bound templates is
conjectural. For guaranteed, exact synthesis the leading coefficient had moved only from
$\tfrac{23}{48}$ (2004) to $\tfrac{22}{48}$ (2024).

\subsection{Contribution}

The contribution is a single free gate. Each node decomposes $U(u\otimes I)$ instead of $U$,
where $u$ is a single-qubit gate on the top qubit; its inverse $u^\dagger$ is placed at the input
of the node and costs no CNOTs (Fig.~\ref{fig:node}). The gate $u$ changes the factor $C$. We
show that with $u=R_z(\alpha)R_y(\beta^\ast)$, for suitable $\alpha$ and $\beta^\ast$, the
eigenphases of $C$ split into two halves $S$ and $S^c$ with equal sums modulo $2\pi$
(Sec.~\ref{sec:exist}). Placing $S$ on the basis states with $j_{n-2}=0$ removes the rotation
between the last two CNOTs of the input-side multiplexor, and both CNOTs fold into the central
factor instead of one (Sec.~\ref{sec:fold}, Fig.~\ref{fig:fold}). The result is an exact
construction with
\[
c(n)=\tfrac{21}{48}4^n-\tfrac32 2^n+2
\]
CNOTs: one fewer per recursion node, a lower leading coefficient, and $18$ CNOTs for a general
three-qubit unitary.
Table~\ref{tab:counts} compares this count with the previous analytic methods and the lower
bound. The saving over block-ZXZ is $(4^{n-2}-1)/3$, one CNOT per recursion node: $1$ for three
qubits, $5$ for four, $85$ for six.

\section{An improved algorithm}

Throughout, $n\ge3$, $D=2^{n-1}$ (so $D\equiv0\bmod4$), and $t=q_{n-1}$ is the top qubit.
Qubits are ordered with $t$ most significant, so $U=\begin{psmallmatrix}X&Y\\ \ast&\ast
\end{psmallmatrix}$ in $D\times D$ blocks. Circuits are drawn left to right in time. Operator
products act right to left.

Block-ZXZ merges one CNOT from each of the two outer multiplexors into the central
multiplexor. In this section we show that the input-side multiplexor can give up a second CNOT,
provided the single-qubit gate on the top qubit at the input of the node is chosen correctly.
This saves one CNOT at every step of the recursion, which results in a total CNOT count of
$\tfrac{21}{48}4^n-\tfrac32 2^n+2$ for decomposing an $n$-qubit unitary.

\subsection{Merging a second CNOT into the central multiplexor}\label{sec:fold}

After the block-ZXZ decomposition, we first demultiplex only the input-side factor $I\oplus C$.
Applying~\eqref{eq:demux} with $U_1=I$ and $U_2=C$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:democ}
\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\0&C\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}V_c&0\\0&V_c\end{pmatrix}\Delta(\theta)
\begin{pmatrix}W_c&0\\0&W_c\end{pmatrix},\qquad
C=V_c\operatorname{diag}\!\big(e^{i\theta_1},\dots,e^{i\theta_D}\big)V_c^\dagger ,
\end{equation}
so the angles $\theta_j$ of the multiplexed rotation are the eigenphases $\mu_1,\dots,\mu_D$ of
$C$, taken modulo $2\pi$. The multiplexor $\Delta(\theta)$ is then decomposed in Gray-code
form~\cite{mottonen}: $D$ rotations $R_z(\varphi_k)$ on $t$, each followed by a CNOT onto $t$.
The rotation angles are the Walsh--Hadamard transform of the $\theta_j$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:walsh}
\varphi_k=\frac1D\sum_{j}(-1)^{\,b_{j}\cdot g_k}\,\theta_j ,
\end{equation}
where $b_j$ is the binary code of $j$, $g_k$ is the $k$-th Gray code word and the dot is the
bitwise inner product. The last CNOT is controlled by the most significant control qubit
$q_{n-2}$, the one before it by $q_0$, and the rotation between them has the angle
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phi}
\varphi=\frac1D\sum_{j}(-1)^{j_{n-2}}\,\theta_j ,
\end{equation}
the Walsh coefficient of the highest control bit $j_{n-2}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:fold}a).

Block-ZXZ moves the Hadamard of the central factor to the other side of the last CNOT, which
turns it into a CZ gate, $(H\otimes I)\,\CX(q_{n-2},t)=\CZ(q_{n-2},t)\,(H\otimes I)$. The rotation
$R_z(\varphi)$ blocks the CNOT before it. If $\varphi=0$, however, the last two CNOTs are adjacent
and \emph{both} can be moved through the Hadamard (Fig.~\ref{fig:fold}b):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:twocz}
(H\otimes I)\,\CX(q_{n-2},t)\,\CX(q_0,t)=\CZ(q_{n-2},t)\,\CZ(q_0,t)\,(H\otimes I)
=\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\0&Z_{q_0}Z_{q_{n-2}}\end{pmatrix}(H\otimes I).
\end{equation}
The two CZ gates form a block-diagonal matrix. They can be merged into the central factor
together with the $(n-1)$-qubit gates $V_c$ and $W_a$, as in block-ZXZ~\cite{zxz}. Including the
single CZ that block-ZXZ already takes from the output-side multiplexor, the new central gate is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:btilde}
\begin{aligned}
\tilde B&=\begin{pmatrix}W_aV_c&0\\0&Z_{q_{n-2}}W_aBV_cZ_{q_0}Z_{q_{n-2}}\end{pmatrix}\\
&=\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\0&Z_{q_{n-2}}\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}W_a&0\\0&W_a\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\0&B\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}V_c&0\\0&V_c\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\0&Z_{q_0}Z_{q_{n-2}}\end{pmatrix},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
and it is demultiplexed as a regular multiplexor using~\eqref{eq:demux}. Its cost does not
change, while the input-side multiplexor now needs $D-2$ CNOTs instead of $D-1$.

\subsection{When is the second merge possible?}

The angles $\theta_j$ cannot be chosen freely: by~\eqref{eq:democ}, $\theta_j\equiv\mu_j\pmod
{2\pi}$. Two choices are left. The first is which eigenvalue is assigned to which basis state
$j$, i.e.\ the order of the columns of $V_c$. The second is the branch of each $\theta_j$:
replacing $\theta_j$ by $\theta_j+2\pi$ flips the sign of one eigenvector, which is absorbed by
$V_c$ and $W_c$. The sum~\eqref{eq:phi} only sees which eigenvalues are placed on the states with
$j_{n-2}=0$, not their order within each half. If a set $S$ of $D/2$ eigenvalues is placed on
$j_{n-2}=0$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phiS}
\varphi=\frac1D\Big(\sum_{k\in S}\mu_k-\sum_{k\notin S}\mu_k\Big)+\frac{2\pi\ell}{D},\qquad
\ell\in\mathbb Z,
\end{equation}
where $\ell$ is set by the branches. So $\varphi$ can be made zero exactly when the eigenphases of
$C$ admit a \emph{balanced split}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:split}
\sum_{k\in S}\mu_k-\sum_{k\notin S}\mu_k\in 2\pi\mathbb Z,\qquad |S|=D/2 .
\end{equation}
Equivalently, the product of the eigenvalues in $S$ equals the product of the eigenvalues
outside $S$. For a generic $C$ no balanced split exists. The condition is a single real equation,
however, so one free real parameter in the node should be enough to meet it.

\subsection{Choosing the top-qubit gate}\label{sec:exist}

A single-qubit gate $u$ on $t$ costs no CNOTs. We decompose $U(u\otimes I)$ instead of $U$ and
place $u^\dagger$ on $t$ at the start of the circuit, where it merges with the neighbouring
single-qubit gates. The gate $u$ changes the top-row blocks of the matrix to be decomposed, and
with them $C$. A phase gate $R_z(\alpha)$ is not enough on its own: it maps
$(X,Y)\mapsto(e^{-i\alpha/2}X,\,e^{i\alpha/2}Y)$, which multiplies $C$ by $e^{i\alpha}$ and leaves
the differences in~\eqref{eq:split} unchanged. An $R_y$ rotation mixes the two blocks, so we use
$u(\beta)=R_z(\alpha)R_y(\beta)$ and follow $C$ along $\beta\in[0,\pi]$. Up to the phases
$e^{\mp i\alpha/2}$, the top-row blocks become
\begin{equation}\label{eq:path}
\begin{pmatrix}X(\beta)&Y(\beta)\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}X&Y\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}c\,I&-s\,I\\ s\,I&c\,I\end{pmatrix},\qquad c=\cos\tfrac\beta2,\ s=\sin\tfrac\beta2 ,
\end{equation}
and $C(\beta)=-i\,U_{X(\beta)}^\dagger U_{Y(\beta)}$ is computed from their polar decompositions as
in~\eqref{eq:zxz}.

\paragraph{The endpoints.} At $\beta=\pi$ the blocks are $(X(\pi),Y(\pi))=(Y,-X)$, so the polar
factors are $U_{X(\pi)}=U_Y$ and $U_{Y(\pi)}=-U_X$. Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:inv}
C(\pi)=-i\,U_Y^\dagger(-U_X)=i\,U_Y^\dagger U_X=\big(-i\,U_X^\dagger U_Y\big)^\dagger=C(0)^{-1}.
\end{equation}
Following the path from $\beta=0$ to $\beta=\pi$ inverts $C$, so every eigenphase changes sign.

\paragraph{A test function.} To detect a balanced split, first remove the overall phase of $C$,
which does not affect~\eqref{eq:split}. Let $r(\beta)$ be a $D$-th root of $\det C(\beta)$ that varies continuously with
$\beta$, write the eigenvalues of $C(\beta)/r(\beta)$ as $e^{i\nu_k(\beta)}$, and set
$\psi_S=\sum_{k\in S}\nu_k$ for each balanced split. Since $\det(C/r)=1$, $\psi_{S^c}\equiv-\psi_S
\pmod{2\pi}$, and
\eqref{eq:split} holds for $S$ exactly when $\sin\psi_S=0$. The product over the
$N/2=\tfrac12\binom{D}{D/2}$ pairs $\{S,S^c\}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:P}
P(\beta)=\prod_{\{S,S^c\}}2\sin\psi_S(\beta),
\end{equation}
vanishes exactly when $C(\beta)$ has a balanced split. Written as a Pfaffian,
$P=\Pf(R-R^{\mathsf T})$, where $R$ is the real orthogonal matrix by which $C/r$ acts on the
$(D/2)$-th exterior power (its eigenvalues are the $e^{\pm i\psi_S}$). This form shows that $P$
has a well-defined sign and is a continuous function of $\beta$.

\paragraph{The sign change.} By~\eqref{eq:inv}, $C(\pi)/r(\pi)$ is the inverse of $C(0)/r(0)$, up
to how far the root $r$ has turned along the way. Write $\tau_1,\dots,\tau_D$ for the eigenvalues
of $e^{i\alpha}X^{-1}Y$, assumed non-real. Along the path,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dets}
\det X(\beta)=\det X\prod_k\big(c+s\,\tau_k\big),\qquad
\det Y(\beta)=\det Y\prod_k\big(c-s/\tau_k\big),
\end{equation}
neither of which vanishes, so $C(\beta)$ is continuous. Counting how far the phase of each factor
turns gives $r(\pi)=\overline{r(0)}\,e^{2\pi i m/D}$ with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:m}
m=\#\{k:\operatorname{Im}\tau_k>0\}-\tfrac D2 .
\end{equation}
Hence $\nu_k(\pi)=-\nu_k(0)-2\pi m/D$ after relabelling, and every $\psi_S$ becomes
$-\psi_S-\pi m$. Each factor of~\eqref{eq:P} is therefore multiplied by $(-1)^{m+1}$. The number
of factors, $N/2=\binom{D-1}{D/2-1}$, is odd because $D-1$ is all ones in binary, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sign}
P(\pi)=(-1)^{m+1}P(0).
\end{equation}
The phase $\alpha$ is used to make $m$ even. As $\alpha$ goes around the circle, each $\tau_k$
does too, and the count in~\eqref{eq:m} changes by one each time a $\tau_k$ crosses the real axis,
so it is even on some interval of $\alpha$. With such an $\alpha$, $P(\pi)=-P(0)$, and $P$ has a
zero $\beta^\ast\in[0,\pi]$. At $u=R_z(\alpha)R_y(\beta^\ast)$ the factor $C$ has a balanced split,
the split $S$ is placed on $j_{n-2}=0$, and the input-side multiplexor gives up two CNOTs.

The argument needs $X$ and $Y$ to be invertible, which fails only on a set of measure zero. The
circuits produced all share one CNOT skeleton. The set of unitaries this skeleton reaches with
arbitrary single-qubit gates is closed and contains all generic $U$, so it contains every $U$.

In practice, $\alpha$ is taken from a grid of values, and $\beta^\ast$ is found by scanning
$[0,\pi]$ for a sign change of $P$ and refining it by bisection, while the eigenphases of $C(\beta)$
and the root $r(\beta)$ are tracked continuously. The rest of the node is decomposed as in
block-ZXZ.

\subsection{Gate count}

For a generic three-qubit unitary, the decomposition results in four generic two-qubit gates.
Three of these need two CNOTs, since each is decomposed up to a diagonal that is merged into the
next, and the last one needs three. The input-side multiplexor now needs two CNOTs, the central
multiplexor four and the output-side multiplexor three. The total CNOT count for a three-qubit
unitary is therefore $3\cdot2+3+2+4+3=18$ CNOT gates, one fewer than block-ZXZ.

For bigger operators we start from the recursive relation. An $n$-qubit unitary requires $c_n$
CNOTs, which are at most
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rec}
c_n\le 4\cdot c_{n-1}-3+3\cdot2^{n-1}-3=4\cdot c_{n-1}+3\cdot2^{n-1}-6 .
\end{equation}
At each level of the recursion, the count is the sum of the CNOTs for the four smaller unitaries
($c_{n-1}$ each) and for the three multiplexors ($2^{n-1}$ each). Three of the smaller unitaries
need one CNOT less because of the diagonal migration, the output-side multiplexor needs one CNOT
less because of the block-ZXZ merge, and the input-side multiplexor needs two CNOTs less because
of Sec.~\ref{sec:fold}. Unrolling~\eqref{eq:rec} down to $c_2\le3$,
\begin{align*}
c_n&\le 4^{n-2}c_2+3\cdot2^{n-1}\big(1+2^{-1}\cdot4+\dots+2^{-(n-3)}\cdot4^{n-3}\big)
-6\big(1+4+\dots+4^{n-3}\big)\\
&= 4^{n-2}c_2+3\cdot2^{n-1}\big(2^{n-2}-1\big)-2\big(4^{n-2}-1\big)\\
&\le\Big(\frac{3}{16}+\frac38-\frac{2}{16}\Big)4^n-\frac32\,2^n+2
=\frac{21}{48}\,4^n-\frac32\,2^n+2 .
\end{align*}
Compared with block-ZXZ, one CNOT is saved at each of the $1+4+\dots+4^{n-3}=(4^{n-2}-1)/3$
recursion nodes.

\section{Results and Discussion}

\subsection{Implementation}



We implemented the construction of Sec.~II in numpy. The two-qubit leaves use the standard
3-CNOT and 2-CNOT-up-to-diagonal constructions. The implementation reproduces the counts of
Table~\ref{tab:counts}: $18$, $90$ and $402$ CNOTs for Haar-random unitaries at $n=3,4,5$, with
reconstruction errors $2\cdot10^{-15}$, $6\cdot10^{-15}$ and $2\cdot10^{-14}$. The sign relation
$P(\pi)=-P(0)$ of~\eqref{eq:sign} held on every sampled target once $\alpha$ was chosen,
and the root search succeeded on $300/300$ random three-qubit targets.

\subsection{Comparison with block-ZXZ}

The construction improves block-ZXZ in the setting it was designed for: exact synthesis of
every unitary with a guaranteed count. The leading coefficient drops from $\tfrac{22}{48}$ to
$\tfrac{21}{48}$, about $4.5\%$ fewer CNOTs asymptotically. The count is lower at every
$n\ge3$, and $18$ CNOTs for a general three-qubit unitary is below the $19$ of the best previous
analytic method. The mechanism is the one block-ZXZ already uses, a CNOT folded into the central
factor as a CZ. The new ingredient is spending the free top-qubit single-qubit gate to make a
second fold possible, with an argument that it always exists rather than a heuristic. The improvement is
modest in absolute terms. The gap to the lower bound ($\tfrac{12}{48}$) remains, and numerical
compilers reach fewer CNOTs for small $n$~\cite{brickwall,squander}, though without closed
forms or guarantees.

\subsection{Limitations}

The construction is not closed form: each node needs a one-dimensional root search. The
sign change~\eqref{eq:sign} guarantees that the search succeeds, and the CNOT count does not depend on how
the root is found. As implemented, however, evaluating the sign of $P$ ranges over all
$\binom{D}{D/2}/2$ balanced splits. That is cheap for $n\le5$ but grows quickly with $n$. An
efficient evaluation of the sign of $P$, or a direct way to find a split that crosses zero,
would remove this classical overhead and is left open.

\subsection{Outlook: a second free gate}

Each node has a second outer multiplexor, $\Delta_a$, on the output side. It has the same fold
condition, for the eigenphases of $A_1A_2^\dagger$ in place of $C$, and a second free gate
$v=R_y(\gamma)R_z(\alpha')$ on $t$ at the output, which also costs no CNOTs. We attempted to extend
Sec.~\ref{sec:exist} to the pair $(u,v)$, i.e.\ to the node $U'=(v\otimes I)\,U\,(u\otimes I)$.
The attempt clarifies the structure but does not yet give a proof.

Write the cosine--sine decomposition as $U'=(L_1\oplus L_2)\begin{psmallmatrix}\Gamma&-\Sigma\\
\Sigma&\Gamma\end{psmallmatrix}(R_1\oplus R_2)$. Inserting it into~\eqref{eq:zxz} gives, up to
similarity,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:csd}
C\sim i\,R_2R_1^\dagger,\qquad A_1A_2^\dagger\sim i\,L_1L_2^\dagger ,
\end{equation}
independent of the cosines $\Gamma$ and sines $\Sigma$. The two fold conditions are therefore
balanced splits of the right and the left halves of the cosine--sine decomposition. The two gates
act on them in a dual way:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dual}
\beta\mapsto\beta+\pi:\ \ C\mapsto C^{-1},\ \ A_1A_2^\dagger\mapsto-A_1A_2^\dagger,\qquad
\gamma\mapsto\gamma+\pi:\ \ C\mapsto-C,\ \ A_1A_2^\dagger\mapsto\big(A_1A_2^\dagger\big)^{-1}.
\end{equation}
A sign flip of the whole spectrum preserves a balanced split, while an inversion flips the sign
of the test function~\eqref{eq:P}, as in~\eqref{eq:sign}. So the test function of $C$ changes sign
across the $\beta$ direction of the $(\beta,\gamma)$ torus and that of $A_1A_2^\dagger$ across the
$\gamma$ direction. If both functions were continuous on the whole torus, these complementary twists
would be expected to force their zero curves to cross, much as two functions that change sign
across opposite pairs of edges of a square must vanish together. That would give both folds at
once.

The obstruction is the set of points where a top-row block of $U'$ is singular. There the polar
factors jump, the phase of $\det C$ winds once around the point, and the test function changes
sign on a small loop around it, so zero curves can end there. The condition on $A_1A_2^\dagger$ is
singular where a left-column block of $U'$ is. Since $X$ and $Y$ have the same singular values as
the bottom-row blocks $U'_{22}$ and $U'_{21}$, the singular points are the same for both
conditions. For some sampled three-qubit targets, a choice of $(\alpha,\alpha')$ removes them
all, but for others every choice we tried leaves between two and six. Numerically, gates meeting
both conditions still exist for every sampled target and give exact $17$-CNOT three-qubit
circuits. Controlling these singular points would save a second CNOT per node and lower the
leading coefficient to $\tfrac{20}{48}$, i.e.\ $\tfrac{20}{48}4^n-\tfrac32 2^n+\tfrac73$ CNOTs.
