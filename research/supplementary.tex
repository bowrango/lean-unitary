Notation follows Sec.~II of the paper. Throughout, $d=2^{n-1}$, $t$ is the top qubit, and $j$
indexes control basis states while $k$ indexes eigenphases. Function names refer to
\texttt{decomposition.py}.

\section*{1. The search problem}

The left-side multiplexor $I\oplus C(g)$ of a recursion node is a Gray-code sequence of $d$
rotations $R_z(\varphi_j)$ on $t$, separated by CX gates. Its angles are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phi}
\boldsymbol\varphi=\tfrac1d H\,\boldsymbol\theta,\qquad
\varphi:=\varphi_{d-1}=\tfrac1d\,\mathbf h^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\theta,\qquad h_j=(-1)^{j_{n-2}},
\end{equation}
where $H$ is the Walsh--Hadamard matrix in Gray-code order. The angles $\boldsymbol\theta$ assign
the eigenphases $\mu_0,\dots,\mu_{d-1}$ of $C$ to the control basis states,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:branch}
\theta_j=\mu_{k(j)}+2\pi m_j,\qquad m_j\in\mathbb Z,
\end{equation}
and both the assignment $j\mapsto k(j)$ and the branches $m_j$ are free. The additional CX gate
merges into the central multiplexor exactly when $\varphi=0$, that is, when the eigenphases admit
a \emph{balanced split}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:split}
S\subset\{0,\dots,d-1\},\quad |S|=\tfrac d2,\qquad
\sum_{k\in S}\mu_k-\sum_{k\notin S}\mu_k=-2\pi\ell\quad\text{for some }\ell\in\mathbb Z .
\end{equation}
The eigenphases in $S$ are then placed on the $+1$ entries of $\mathbf h$ and the rest on the $-1$
entries.

\paragraph{Why a fixed $C$ does not work.} For a fixed $C$, finding $S$ is an equal-cardinality
number-partitioning problem modulo $2\pi$: split $d$ numbers into two halves of equal size and
equal sum. The integer version is NP-complete, and a direct check visits all
$\tfrac12\binom{d}{d/2}\sim 2^d/\sqrt{2\pi d}$ splits, which is doubly exponential in $n$. Worse,
for generic real eigenphases no balanced split exists at all, since~\eqref{eq:split} is one real
equation with no free parameter.

\paragraph{What the pivot does.} The pivot $g=R_z(\alpha)R_y(\beta)$ on $t$ supplies the missing
freedom. It changes $C$ continuously, and instead of searching over splits, the construction
\emph{fixes} the split in advance, the middle half of the sorted eigenphases, and moves $C$ until
that split balances. The search over $\binom{d}{d/2}$ subsets becomes two one-dimensional
searches (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}):
\begin{enumerate}
\item choose $\alpha^\ast$ so that the path $\beta\mapsto C(\beta)$, $\beta\in[0,\pi]$, is well
  defined and ends at $C(\pi)=C(0)^{-1}$;
\item along this path, the imbalance of the middle-half split changes sign, so a root search
  in $\beta$ finds $\beta^\ast$ where it vanishes.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{2. Implementation}

Write $U=\begin{psmallmatrix}X&Y\\ \ast&\ast\end{psmallmatrix}$ in $d\times d$ blocks, with $X,Y$
invertible, which holds for generic $U$. The pivot gives $U(g\otimes I)$ the top-row blocks
\begin{equation}\label{eq:path}
X_g=e^{-i\alpha/2}c\,X+e^{i\alpha/2}s\,Y,\qquad
Y_g=-e^{-i\alpha/2}s\,X+e^{i\alpha/2}c\,Y,\qquad c=\cos\tfrac\beta2,\ s=\sin\tfrac\beta2 .
\end{equation}
The search is \texttt{find\_pivot}, and the node is then synthesized by \texttt{synth}.

\paragraph{Step 0: direct pivots.} Before searching, \texttt{find\_pivot} tries $\alpha=0$ with
$\beta=0$ and $\beta=\pi/2$. If either already admits a balanced split (checked as in Step~5), it
is used. This is a shortcut that also covers simple singular blocks such as $Y=0$; the generic
case falls through to Steps~1--4.

\paragraph{Step 1: choosing $\alpha$.} The path must keep $X_g$ and $Y_g$ invertible, and its
determinant phase must end where it started, negated. Both conditions are controlled by the
eigenvalues of $X^{-1}Y$ after a rotation by $\alpha$. Let $\rho_k$ be the eigenvalues of
$X^{-1}Y$, so that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:N}
\tau_k=e^{i\alpha}\rho_k,\qquad N_\pm(\alpha)=\#\{k:\pm\operatorname{Im}\tau_k>0\}.
\end{equation}
If no $\tau_k$ is real, then $X_g$ and $Y_g$ stay invertible for all $\beta\in[0,\pi]$, and if in
addition $N_+=N_-=d/2$, then the continuous determinant phase satisfies $\sigma(\pi)=-\sigma(0)$
(Step~3). $N_+$ is piecewise constant in $\alpha$ and changes only at the crossing angles
$\alpha\equiv-\arg\rho_k\pmod\pi$, where one $\tau_k$ crosses the real axis.
\texttt{\_choose\_alpha} sorts these angles, evaluates $N_+$ on each interval between them, and
returns $\alpha^\ast$, the midpoint of the widest interval with $N_+=d/2$. Taking the midpoint
keeps every $\tau_k$ as far from the real axis as possible. Cost: one eigendecomposition and one
sort.

\paragraph{Step 2: evaluating $C(\beta)$.} With $\alpha=\alpha^\ast$ fixed, the Block-ZXZ
multiplexor matrix is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Cdef}
C(\beta)=-i\,X_g^\dagger\,(X_gX_g^\dagger)^{-1/2}(Y_gY_g^\dagger)^{-1/2}\,Y_g=-i\,Q_X^\dagger Q_Y,
\end{equation}
where $X_g=P_XQ_X$ and $Y_g=P_YQ_Y$ are polar decompositions, with $P_X=(X_gX_g^\dagger)^{1/2}$ and
$P_Y=(Y_gY_g^\dagger)^{1/2}$ positive definite and $Q_X,Q_Y$ unitary. Computing two polar
decompositions per trial is unnecessary. Because $X_gX_g^\dagger+Y_gY_g^\dagger=I$, the factors
$P_X$ and $P_Y$ commute, so
\[
X_g^\dagger Y_g=Q_X^\dagger P_XP_YQ_Y=\big(Q_X^\dagger P_XP_YQ_X\big)\,Q_X^\dagger Q_Y
\]
is a positive definite matrix times $Q_X^\dagger Q_Y$. Hence $Q_X^\dagger Q_Y$ is the unitary polar
factor of the single matrix $X_g^\dagger Y_g$. Substituting~\eqref{eq:path}, this matrix is affine
in $(\cos\beta,\sin\beta)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:M}
X_g^\dagger Y_g=A_-+\cos\beta\,A_++\sin\beta\,B,\qquad
A=e^{i\alpha}X^\dagger Y,\quad A_\pm=\tfrac{A\pm A^\dagger}2,\quad
B=\tfrac{Y^\dagger Y-X^\dagger X}2,
\end{equation}
so $A_\pm$ and $B$ are computed once per node. Each trial then costs one SVD,
$X_g^\dagger Y_g=L\Sigma R$, giving $C(\beta)=-i\,LR$ (\texttt{unitary\_polar}), followed by one
eigensolve of $C(\beta)$. The eigensolve (\texttt{unitary\_phases}) diagonalizes the Hermitian
matrix $\tfrac12(C+C^\dagger)+\tfrac{\gamma}{2i}(C-C^\dagger)$ with $\gamma=(\sqrt5-1)/2$, which
shares the eigenvectors of $C$ and returns them orthonormal; near-degenerate clusters are resolved
by a small dense eigensolve.

\paragraph{Step 3: lifting the eigenphases.} The eigensolver returns principal eigenphases
$\mu_k\in(-\pi,\pi]$, which jump by $2\pi$ whenever an eigenvalue crosses $-1$. A root search
needs a version that varies continuously with $\beta$. The construction uses the continuous
phase $\sigma(\beta)$ of $\det C(\beta)$, which can be tracked analytically. The phase of
$\det C$ is that of $\det Y_g/\det X_g=\prod_k\tau_k(c-s/\tau_k)/(c+s\tau_k)$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sigma}
\sigma(\beta)=\sum_k\Big(\arg\tau_k+\arg\big(c-s/\tau_k\big)-\arg\big(c+s\tau_k\big)\Big),
\qquad \sigma(0)=\sum_k\arg\tau_k,
\end{equation}
satisfies $e^{i\sigma}=\det C$. It is continuous because, for nonreal $\tau_k$, both factors stay
in the open half-plane containing $\tau_k$, and the choice $N_+(\alpha^\ast)=d/2$ gives
$\sigma(\pi)=-\sigma(0)$.

The lifted eigenphases $\nu_k(\beta)$ are the unique real representatives of the eigenphases,
ordered by rank $k$, that fit in one window of width $2\pi$ and have sum $\sigma$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nu}
\nu_0(\beta)\le\cdots\le\nu_{d-1}(\beta)\le\nu_0(\beta)+2\pi,\qquad
\sum_k\nu_k(\beta)=\sigma(\beta).
\end{equation}
\texttt{ordered\_phases} computes them directly from the sorted $\mu_k$. Every list satisfying the
window condition is a cyclic shift of the sorted list: one shift moves the smallest phase to the
end after adding $2\pi$, which raises the sum by exactly $2\pi$. The code therefore applies
\[
r=\operatorname{round}\Big(\frac{\sigma-\sum_k\mu_k}{2\pi}\Big)
\]
shifts. Since the two sums differ by an integer multiple of $2\pi$, an error in the analytic
$\sigma$ smaller than $\pi$ does not change $r$; the code asserts that they agree modulo $2\pi$ to
within $\pi/2$, and the lifted values themselves come from the eigensolver. The list is thus
continuous in $\beta$ without tracking eigenvalues from one trial to the next.

\paragraph{Step 4: choosing $\beta$.} Fix the middle half of the ranks,
$S=\{d/4,\dots,3d/4-1\}$, and define the imbalance
\begin{equation}\label{eq:P}
P(\beta)=\sum_{k\in S}\nu_k(\beta)-\sum_{k\notin S}\nu_k(\beta).
\end{equation}
A root $P(\beta^\ast)=0$ is a balanced split~\eqref{eq:split} with $\mu_k=\nu_k(\beta^\ast)$ and
$\ell=0$. A root always exists in $[0,\pi]$: since $C(\pi)=C(0)^{-1}$, the eigenphases at
$\beta=\pi$ are the negatives of those at $\beta=0$, and with $\sigma(\pi)=-\sigma(0)$ the lifted
list satisfies $\nu_k(\pi)=-\nu_{d-1-k}(0)$, the reversed negative. The middle half $S$ is
invariant under $k\mapsto d-1-k$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sign}
P(\pi)=-P(0),
\end{equation}
and $P$ changes sign on $[0,\pi]$.

$P$ is continuous but only piecewise smooth: it has a kink wherever an eigenphase crosses the
boundary of $S$. The code finds $\beta^\ast$ with Brent's method (\texttt{scipy.optimize.brentq}).
Like bisection, it keeps a sign-change bracket, so it always converges to a root; unlike
bisection, it takes secant and inverse-quadratic steps where $P$ is smooth, so it needs far fewer
trials. (The code evaluates $P/2$, which has the same root.)

\paragraph{Step 5: demultiplexing.} At $\beta^\ast$, \texttt{demux\_double\_fold} demultiplexes
$C$ as in~\eqref{eq:demux_C} below, which returns principal eigenphases rather than the lifted
$\nu_k$. The lifted list is a cyclic shift of the sorted principal eigenphases, so its middle half
is a contiguous window of them in circular order. \texttt{cyclic\_split} therefore checks only the
$d/2$ windows of length $d/2$, not all subsets, and keeps the one with the smallest residual
in~\eqref{eq:split}. These eigenphases are placed on the basis states with $h_j=+1$, the rest on
$h_j=-1$, in
\begin{equation}\label{eq:demux_C}
C(g)=V\operatorname{diag}\big(e^{i\theta_0},\dots,e^{i\theta_{d-1}}\big)V^\dagger .
\end{equation}
With principal rather than lifted values, the split balances only up to a possibly nonzero
$\ell$ in~\eqref{eq:split}, so $\varphi=-2\pi\ell/d$. The code removes this by the branch shift
$\theta_0\mapsto\theta_0+2\pi\ell$ ($m_0=\ell$ in~\eqref{eq:branch}); when $\ell$ is odd this
flips the sign of the corresponding eigenvector, which is absorbed into the first row of $W$.
The result is $\varphi=0$ exactly.

\paragraph{Cost.} Each trial costs $O(d^3)$ time and $O(d^2)$ memory, and the number of trials per
node is bounded. No step enumerates splits.

\section*{3. Experiments}

\texttt{python decomposition.py 3 4 5 6} synthesizes one Haar-random unitary for each $n$ and
checks that the CX count matches the closed form
$c_n=\tfrac{21}{48}4^n-\tfrac32 2^n+2$ and that the circuit, followed by the returned diagonal,
reproduces $U$ to within $10^{-8}$ in every entry.

\texttt{benchmark.py} compares the construction with Block-ZXZ on the same Haar-random unitaries
for $n=3,\dots,10$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmark}). Both CX counts match their closed forms at every
$n$. Both methods share the same two-qubit leaf synthesis, and the timing covers the complete
synthesis. The pivot search makes synthesis slower by a roughly constant factor, since every node
stays $O(d^3)$ and the search adds only a bounded number of trials per node.
