\section*{1. The search problem}

The left-side multiplexor of a node is a Gray-code sequence of $D=2^{n-1}$ rotations
$R_z(\varphi_k)$ on the top qubit $t$, separated by CX gates, with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phi}
\boldsymbol\varphi=\tfrac1D H_D\,\boldsymbol\theta,\qquad
\varphi:=\varphi_{D-1}=\tfrac1D\,\mathbf h^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\theta,\qquad h_j=(-1)^{j_{n-2}},
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol\theta$ are the eigenphases of the multiplexor matrix $C$, in any order and up to
$2\pi$ each. The last two CX gates merge into the central factor when $\varphi=0$, that is, when the
eigenphases $\mu_k$ of $C$ admit a \emph{balanced split}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:split}
S\subset\{0,\dots,D-1\},\quad |S|=\tfrac D2,\qquad
\sum_{k\in S}\mu_k-\sum_{k\notin S}\mu_k\in2\pi\mathbb Z .
\end{equation}
For a fixed $C$ this is an equal-cardinality number-partitioning problem, a constrained form of
subset sum, posed modulo $2\pi$: split $D$ numbers into two halves of equal size and equal sum.
The integer version is NP-complete, and the direct check visits all
$\tfrac12\binom{D}{D/2}\sim 2^D/\sqrt{2\pi D}$ splits, which is doubly exponential in $n$. For
continuous data and a fixed $C$, a solution generically does not exist at all.

The pivot $g=R_z(\alpha)R_y(\beta)$ on $t$ replaces this search by two one-dimensional ones. It
changes $C$ continuously, and for a suitable path it guarantees that one \emph{fixed} split, the
middle half of the sorted eigenphases, balances somewhere along it (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}).

\section*{2. Implementation}

All quantities below are computed by \texttt{stage2/foldzxz\_opt.py}. Write
$U=\begin{psmallmatrix}X&Y\\ \ast&\ast\end{psmallmatrix}$ in $D\times D$ blocks, with $X,Y$
invertible.

\paragraph{Choosing $\alpha$.} Let $\rho_k$ be the eigenvalues of $X^{-1}Y$, and define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:N}
\tau_k=e^{i\alpha}\rho_k,\qquad N(\alpha)=\#\{k:\operatorname{Im}\tau_k>0\}.
\end{equation}
$N$ is constant except at the crossing angles $\alpha\equiv-\arg\rho_k\pmod\pi$, where one $\tau_k$
crosses the real axis. The code sorts these angles, evaluates $N$ on each interval between them,
and returns $\alpha^\ast$, the midpoint of the widest interval with $N=D/2$. This keeps every
$\tau_k$ off the real axis, so $X_g$ and $Y_g$ stay invertible along the path. Cost: one
eigendecomposition and one sort.

\paragraph{Evaluating $C(\beta)$.} With $\alpha=\alpha^\ast$ fixed, the product of the top-row
blocks is affine in $(\cos\beta,\sin\beta)$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:M}
X_g^\dagger Y_g=K+\cos\beta\,H+\sin\beta\,B,\qquad
A=e^{i\alpha}X^\dagger Y,\quad H=\tfrac{A+A^\dagger}2,\quad K=\tfrac{A-A^\dagger}2,\quad
B=\tfrac{Y^\dagger Y-X^\dagger X}2 .
\end{equation}
The multiplexor matrix is $C(\beta)=-i\,U_{X_g}^\dagger U_{Y_g}$, and since the positive polar
factors of $X_g$ and $Y_g$ commute, $U_{X_g}^\dagger U_{Y_g}$ is the unitary polar factor of
$X_g^\dagger Y_g$. Each trial therefore costs one SVD, $X_g^\dagger Y_g=L\Sigma R$, giving
$C(\beta)=-i\,LR$, followed by one eigensolve of $C(\beta)$.

\paragraph{Lifting the eigenphases.} The total phase of $\det C$ is tracked analytically,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sigma}
\sigma(\beta)=\sum_k\Big(t_k+\arg\big(c-s/\tau_k\big)-\arg\big(c+s\tau_k\big)\Big),\qquad
t_k=\arg\tau_k,\quad c=\cos\tfrac\beta2,\ s=\sin\tfrac\beta2,
\end{equation}
which is continuous because each factor stays in the half-plane of $\tau_k$, and satisfies
$e^{i\sigma}=\det C$, $\sigma(\pi)=-\sigma(0)$ for $\alpha=\alpha^\ast$. The principal eigenphases are
sorted, and the lifted list is a cyclic shift of them, each shift raising the smallest phase by
$2\pi$ and the sum by $2\pi$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nu}
\nu_0(\beta)\le\dots\le\nu_{D-1}(\beta)\le\nu_0(\beta)+2\pi,\qquad\sum_j\nu_j(\beta)=\sigma(\beta).
\end{equation}
The analytic $\sigma$ is used only to choose the number of shifts, the integer closest to
$(\sigma-\sum_j\mu_j)/2\pi$, so it may be inaccurate by anything less than $\pi$; the lifted values
themselves come from the eigenphases. This makes the list unique and continuous in $\beta$, with no
tracking between trials.

\paragraph{Choosing $\beta$.} With $S=\{D/4,\dots,3D/4-1\}$, the middle half of the list, the
imbalance is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:P}
P(\beta)=\sum_{j\in S}\nu_j(\beta)-\sum_{j\notin S}\nu_j(\beta).
\end{equation}
Since $C(\pi)=C(0)^{-1}$, the list at $\beta=\pi$ is the reversed negative of the list at $\beta=0$,
and $S$ is invariant under $j\mapsto D-1-j$, so $P(\pi)=-P(0)$ and $[0,\pi]$ brackets a root. $P$ is
continuous but only piecewise smooth: it has a kink wherever an eigenphase crosses the boundary of
$S$. The code finds $\beta^\ast$ with $P(\beta^\ast)=0$ by Brent's method, which keeps a sign-change
bracket, so it is as safe as bisection, while taking secant and inverse-quadratic steps where $P$ is
smooth, so it needs far fewer trials.

\paragraph{Demultiplexing.} At $\beta^\ast$, the middle half of the lifted list is a contiguous
window of the principal eigenphases in circular order, so it is recovered by checking the $D/2$
windows of length $D/2$, not all subsets. These eigenvalues are placed on $j_{n-2}=0$, and a $2\pi$
shift of one angle, with the matching sign flip in $W_c$, removes any remaining multiple of
$2\pi/D$ from $\varphi$. The cost per node is $O(D^3)$ per trial and $O(D^2)$ memory, with no
enumeration of splits.

\section*{3. Experiments}

\texttt{foldzxz\_opt.py} synthesizes Haar-random unitaries for $n=3,4,5$ and checks, for each, that
the CX count matches the formula $c(n)=\tfrac{21}{48}4^n-\tfrac32 2^n+2$ and that the circuit
reproduces $U$ to machine precision, up to a global phase. The figures below follow one
Haar-random target with $n=4$ through the construction, and are produced by
\texttt{stage2/balanced\_figs.py}. The middle half $S$ is drawn in \textcolor{cmid}{blue} and the
outer quarters in \textcolor{cout}{orange}.
